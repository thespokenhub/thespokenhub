## copy 3

Huge mistake when writing:
Making a statement and moving on without explaining WHY.
People do this because they falsely assume the reader will "get it" without more context.
This leads to two potential outcomes:
1. People don't get it, so they leave cuz they're confused
2. People think they get it, so they misunderstand your point because their assumption is wrong
Neither outcome is good. You want people to read your content, so leaving is a missed opportunity.
But if people misunderstand your point, you'll:
-Struggle to build the emotional resonance you intended (you weren't specific enough, so you're not on the same ride—which means you won't end up in the same place at the end)
-Struggle to build meaningful connections (because you're on different rides and get off in different places, you aren't positioned to get to know each other better)
-Struggle to get people to take action (it's like trying to sell a mindset shift that will make your ride 10x better to people that haven't been on it in the first place)
Here's a specific example:
Statement: "Startup fundraising is hard."
There are a million reasons why this can be hard, like:
-Unproven business success
-Prospects don't understand how they'll get a return on investment
-Pitching to the wrong prospects
As the reader, if you don't tell me from your point of view why it's hard, I will make my own assumption.
Let's say I assume it's hard because finding the right prospects to pitch is a pain.
But your specialty is helping entrepreneurs better explain to prospects how they'll get an ROI.

That's not super helpful if I can't get investors to take a meeting.

I may need this help down the line, but right now, I'm annoyed cuz I clicked into something that isn't solving my current problem.

If the writer had been more specific, they would have attracted more readers that aligned with their ideal customer profile (ICP) and fewer that didn't.

And/or, convinced "not ideal right now" readers to bookmark the advice for later, instead of luring them in and leaving them unhelped. This is the "unaware" audience segment. They don't know they'll probably have this problem, but they fit your ICP, so you want them to think of you in the future.

So here's a better way to write the above statement:

Statement: "Startup fundraising is hard."
WHY: "With little to no revenue to show for, it's an uphill battle convincing investors they'll get a lucrative return on investment."

AHHHH, OKKKKKKKKKK.

Now we know where the author is coming from and where they're going next.

Ideal audiences will nod along, non-ideal audiences will move along, and future audiences will bookmark for later.
The best possible outcome.

## copy 8

Here's my problem with most writing advice on social:

On its face, it promotes thinking for yourself. But when you dig deeper, you realize the advice-giver is using the same tired templates.

It's why so many "writers" here sound the same.

I'm using quotes because I classify good writers as those that think for themselves.

Anyone can learn how to put words in an order that resonates.

But if you aren't saying anything new (i.e. adding your POV, your experiences, your leading thoughts) it's not writing—it's amplifying.

Amplifiers have a low-barrier to entry. All you need to do is read a few writing books, buy a few low-ticket courses, follow a few big accounts, and promote those ideas.

I watch tons of people grow and make money this way.

Good for them but this won't help you, the buyer, write better or achieve your goals.

Because the best way to stand out and grow your business is to differentiate.

You cannot do that if you buy a thing from someone that teaches you to sound like them (and the 100 people they also sound like).

It's very hard to distinguish writers from amplifiers.

## copy 11

Social writing 101

A great hook answers:

-How will this help me?
-What will I learn?
-Is this worth my time?

A great body answers:

-Why should I care?
-How does this work?
-How can I do it myself?
-What's my next step?

You need a BOTH to build trust & generate demand.

## copy 16

Notice how when you read a hook that says…

This [tip/hack/advice] is gonna change your life…

It never does?

But why?
The reason is that the person who wrote that was lazy.

Instead of drilling down their audience's needs and wants?

"Change your life".

They're setting high expectations.
And not delivering by default because they're not specific.

Makes sense?

So here's the lesson:

• Promise less
• Research more
• Don't be afraid to go SUPER specific.

Never in the writing.
