## 003 · Writers before beliefs
**Mode:** A · **For:** founders · **Graphic:** none

---
Huge mistake founders make with content:

Hiring writers before they know what they believe.

A writer can make your point sharper. They can't make it up for you.

So when there's no point, you get 40 blog posts that politely agree with everyone. Nice formatting. Zero reason to pick you.

Before you brief anyone, answer three questions:

1. What do we believe that our competitors don't?
2. Who loses if we're right?
3. What would a customer say we're wrong about?

If you can answer all three, you have a content strategy. Hire the writer.

If you can't, you don't need a writer yet.

You need a long walk and a notebook.

## 006 · The 3 questions
**Mode:** A · **For:** content managers, founders · **Graphic:** framework

---
Most B2B posts fail before the first word is written.

Not because the writing is bad. Because nobody decided what the piece is supposed to DO.

Before you write anything, answer these:

1. Who is this for? (And who is it NOT for?)
2. What do they believe before they read it?
3. What should they believe after?

Number 3 is the one everyone skips.

And it's the only one that matters, because "after" is where the deal, the sign-up, or the forwarded link lives.

If you can't answer #3, you didn't write content.

You wrote an essay. Lovely hobby. Bad strategy.

I put all three in the graphic. Steal it for your next brief.

## 009 · Customers write the best headlines
**Mode:** A · **For:** founders, PMMs, content managers · **Graphic:** before/after

---
Your customers already wrote your best headline.

Founders describe the product the way they pitch it.
Customers describe it the way they FEEL it.

Founder: "AI-powered workflow orchestration platform."
Customer: "It stopped me copy-pasting between four tabs all day."

Guess which one makes a buyer stop scrolling.

Three questions to steal on your next customer call:

-What were you using before us?
-What almost stopped you from buying?

Write down their EXACT words. Not your summary. Their words.

That's your copy. You're welcome. 🫡

## 013 · Traffic is not pipeline
**Mode:** A · **For:** founders, growth leads · **Graphic:** none

---
Your blog can get tons of traffic and close zero deals.

Here's WHY that happens:

Easy, high-volume keywords attract students, competitors, and people who will never, ever buy from you. Great for the dashboard. Useless for the pipeline.

Meanwhile, the people who are actually comparing options are searching for things like:

-"[Your category] for [their industry]"
-"[Competitor] alternative"
-"How to [painful job] without [thing they hate]"

Tiny search volume. Huge intent.

Before you commission your next post, ask:

1. Would someone with budget read this?
2. Would sales send it to a prospect?
3. Does it describe the problem the way customers describe it?

Three no's? It might still be a nice post.

It's just not a business post.

## 016 · Edit in 4 passes
**Mode:** A · **For:** writers, content managers · **Graphic:** framework

---
Most people edit in one pass.

That's why their edits are mostly commas.

You can't fix the argument, the structure, AND the sentences at the same time. Your brain will pick the easiest one (sentences) and pretend it did all three.

So edit in passes:

1. The argument pass. Is there ONE clear point? Could you say it in a sentence?
2. The structure pass. Does every section earn its place, in this order?
3. The line pass. Cut filler, swap weak verbs, kill "very."
4. The read-aloud pass. If you trip over it, so will they.

Four passes sounds slow.

It's faster than three rounds of "can we tighten this up?" from your boss.
---

## 023 · The "so what?" test
**Mode:** A · **For:** founders, PMMs · **Graphic:** flow

---
The fastest way to fix boring B2B copy:

Ask "so what?" until it hurts.

Watch:

"Our platform has real-time dashboards."
So what?

"You can see problems as they happen."
So what?

"You catch a churn risk before the renewal call, not after."

OKKKKK. THAT'S the sentence.

Features are where you start. They're not where the reader cares.

Most copy stops at the first answer because it feels finished. It isn't. It's just accurate.

Keep asking until you hit something a buyer would lose sleep over.

Then start there.
---

## 026 · The beige test
**Mode:** A · **For:** founders, CMOs, content managers · **Graphic:** checklist

---
B2B content has a beige problem.

Everything is "fine." Nothing is memorable.

Signs your content is beige:

-You could swap your logo for a competitor's and nobody would notice
-Your "contrarian take" is that quality matters
-Every post ends with "In conclusion"
-Your intro starts with "In today's fast-paced world"
-Legal, product, and sales all approved it without a single comment (suspicious)

Here's WHY it happens: when ten people review a post, each one sands off a little edge. By the end it's smooth, safe, and says nothing.

The fix is uncomfortable, butttt simple:

Say one thing your competitor wouldn't sign their name to.

Then protect it in review like it's your firstborn.
---

## 029 · Publish is not distribution
**Mode:** A · **For:** content managers, growth leads, founders · **Graphic:** framework

---
"Hit publish" is not a distribution strategy.

It's a hope.

Most teams spend 90% of the effort writing a piece and 10% telling anyone it exists. Then they wonder why it "didn't perform."

One good piece should show up at least five times:

1. A founder post on LinkedIn (the opinion, not the link)
2. A snippet in a sales follow-up email
3. A line in the customer newsletter
4. An answer in a community where your buyers hang out
5. An update to an older post that already ranks

Write once.
Show up five times.

Content is king, sure.

But distribution is the kingdom, and a lot of kings are out here broke.

## 032 · Briefs
**Mode:** A · **For:** content managers, CMOs · **Graphic:** none

---
A bad brief costs you two drafts.

Minimum.

Most briefs are a keyword, a word count, and "make it engaging."

That's not a brief. That's a ransom note.

A good brief answers five things:

1. Who exactly is reading this? (One person, not "marketers")
2. What do they believe right now?
3. What do we want them to believe after?
4. What's the one example or insight only WE can use?
5. What are we deliberately leaving out?

Number 5 saves the most time. Nobody writes it.

Thirty minutes on the brief saves you three rounds of "this isn't quite what I had in mind."

Which, by the way, is what people say when they never had anything in mind.

## 035 · Hooks without clickbait
**Mode:** A · **For:** amplifiers, founders · **Graphic:** none

---
Your hook makes a promise.

Your post has to keep it.

That's why "This will change your life" never does. It promises everything and gets specific about nothing.

Three hooks that work without clickbait:

1. The specific number.
"I rewrote one landing page headline 23 times. Here's the one that won."

2. The named mistake.
"Huge mistake when writing case studies: cutting the part where things went wrong."

3. The uncomfortable question.
"If your blog disappeared tomorrow, would sales notice?"

What they have in common: you know exactly what you're getting.

Promise less. Be specific. Then over-deliver in the body.

Lower expectations, happier readers. Works in relationships too, apparently.

## 038 · Case studies that aren't brags
**Mode:** A · **For:** PMMs, content managers, founders · **Graphic:** framework

---
Most case studies read like a humblebrag in a nice font.

"Company X used our tool and achieved amazing results."

Cool. I don't believe you.

Here's a structure that does get believed:

1. The before, in the customer's words (the messy, honest version)
2. The moment they decided to change (what broke?)
3. What they tried first that DIDN'T work
4. What changed when they switched
5. What's different now (one number, one quote)

Number 3 is the most persuasive part.

Everyone cuts it.

It's the bit where your buyer goes "oh, that's literally us," because they've tried the same thing and failed too.

Don't sell to people trying for the first time. Sell to the ones who already got burned.
---

## 041 · Founder-led content without writing
**Mode:** A · **For:** founders · **Graphic:** none

---
"I don't have time to write."

Fair. You don't need to.

You need to talk for 20 minutes a week.

Here's WHY founder content matters even if you hate writing: buyers trust people more than logos, and you're the only one who knows why the company exists. Your marketing team can explain WHAT you sell. Only you can explain WHY.

The lazy (smart) way:

1. Once a week, record a voice note answering one question a customer asked you
2. Get it transcribed
3. Hand it to a writer or editor whose only job is finding the ONE sharp idea in there
4. Post it under your name

Twenty minutes of rambling usually holds two or three good posts.

Your rambling is more valuable than you think.

(Most of it. Not the part about your fantasy league.)

## 044 · Measuring content honestly
**Mode:** A · **For:** CMOs, founders, growth leads · **Graphic:** comparison table

---
Most content dashboards are designed to make someone feel good.

Usually the person who built the dashboard.

Here's how to tell if you're measuring content or just admiring it:

Page views → vs → assisted conversions
Likes → vs → DMs from actual buyers
Time on page → vs → sales asking for the link
Posts published → vs → posts sales actually uses
Rankings → vs → demo requests from organic

The left column isn't useless. It just answers "did people see it?"

The right column answers "did it matter?"

Track what makes your CFO lean in, not what makes the dashboard pretty.

## 047 · The quote after the interview
**Mode:** A · **For:** content managers, writers, PMMs · **Graphic:** none

---
The best quote in a customer interview almost never comes during the interview.

It comes right after you say, "Okay, that's everything, thank you so much."

That's when people relax. The PR voice switches off. And they say the real thing.

"Honestly? We almost went with [competitor] and I'm SO glad we didn't, because…"

Gold. Pure gold.

So:

-Keep recording until the call actually ends
-Ask "What didn't I ask that I should have?"
-After every answer, wait three seconds before you talk. People fill silence with the good stuff.

The interview is the warm-up.

The goodbye is the content.
